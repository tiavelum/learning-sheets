Logging in: the facts

At the entrance: first unlock your phone, then open the door
Device passcode
 PIN, pattern or password that unlocks your device (Android: screen lock); protects its data. Watched as you type it, then the device is stolen. Forgotten: the device usually has to be erased and restored.
Biometrics
 Fingerprint or face check that stands in for the passcode; the scan never leaves the device. Anyone who knows your passcode can add their own finger or face. If it fails, the device asks for the passcode instead.
Password manager
 App that makes, stores and fills in passwords, often passkeys too (e.g. Apple Passwords). Its master password opens everything; a weak one is a risk. Master password forgotten: some managers cannot recover the vault.
Password
 Secret you type; a well-run site keeps only a scrambled check value (hash) of it. Phished, guessed or leaked; reused, it opens your other accounts. Use "Forgot password?" to get a reset link by email or text.
Passkey
 Key pair: the site keeps the public key; your device keeps the private one, used after unlock. Synced ones are only as safe as the account that syncs them. Synced passkeys come back on a new device through your account.
Then: pass the doorman (two-step login)
SMS code
 One-time code sent by text message to your phone number; valid for a few minutes. Fake sites ask for it; thieves can move your number to their SIM. Ask your mobile provider for a new SIM with your number.
Authenticator app
 App (e.g. Microsoft Authenticator) that makes a new code about every 30 s, offline. A fake site can ask for the code and use it at once. The codes are gone unless the app's backup or sync was on.
Sign-in prompt
 Notice from the service's app asking you to approve a sign-in; also called push approval. Attackers send prompt after prompt, hoping you tap Yes. Approve on another signed-in device, or use another way back in.
Security key
 USB or NFC device (e.g. YubiKey) that does a passkey's job; you touch it to confirm. Resists fake sites, but only helps when you have it with you. Register a second key in advance and keep it somewhere safe.
If something is lost: get back in
Recovery email and phone
 Second email address and phone number saved in your account for reset links and codes. Whoever controls them can reset your account; outdated ones fail. Update them as soon as your address or number changes.
Recovery codes
 One-time codes (often ten) you get when turning on two-step login; also called backup codes. Anyone who finds them can use them with your password. While still signed in, create a new set; the old one stops working.
Second device
 Another phone, tablet or computer already signed in (Apple calls it a trusted device). It stays signed in, so it needs its own passcode. Remove it from the account's device list, then sign it out.
Account recovery
 The service's last-resort check: questions about you and the account, often a waiting time. Slow and may fail; free services rarely have a person to call. If it fails, the account and its data may be gone for good.

Ask of every way in

  • Where is it kept: in your head, on your device, or at the service?
  • Could a fake site get it?
  • How would you get back in without it?

Rules to remember

  • One password per account, kept by a password manager.
  • Use passkeys where offered; many sites then skip the second step.
  • Turn on two-step login, first for your email: it can reset the rest.
  • Never read out a code or tap Yes for a sign-in you did not start.
  • Set up at least two ways back in before you need them.
