# Item What it is What it does When
Drawn in the town picture
Antigen
 Part of a germ that immune cells recognise, often a surface protein. Every antibody and T cell the vaccine trains is aimed at it. Vaccination day
Adjuvant
 Ingredient added to many non-live vaccines, e.g. aluminium salts. Alerts first-line immune cells at the injection site for a stronger response. First hours
Dendritic cell
 Immune cell in skin and tissues that takes up antigen. Carries it to the nearest lymph node and shows its fragments to T cells. Hours to days
Lymph node
 Bean-shaped filter on the lymph vessels, e.g. in the armpit. Where the rare T and B cells that fit the antigen switch on and multiply. Days to weeks
Helper T cell
 T cell (CD4) whose receptor fits a fragment of the antigen. Gives B cells and killer T cells the signals to respond fully and form memory. Days
B cell
 White blood cell with one kind of antibody on its surface. Becomes a plasma cell that releases antibodies; their fit improves over weeks. 1 to 4 weeks
Antibody
 Y-shaped protein that binds one antigen. Blocks the germ from entering cells; marks it for scavenger (sweeper) cells. From 1 to 2 weeks; falls slowly
Killer T cell
 T cell (CD8) that spots antigen fragments on infected cells. Kills infected cells, where a virus is out of the antibodies' reach. About 1 to 3 weeks
Herd immunity
 Indirect protection when enough people around are immune. Breaks chains of infection, sparing people who cannot be vaccinated. While enough stay immune
Show: four common kinds of vaccine
Live-attenuated vaccine
 Whole germ, weakened, e.g. against measles or yellow fever. Multiplies a little; strong, lasting. Not in pregnancy or severe immune deficiency. Vaccination day
Inactivated vaccine
 Whole germ, killed, e.g. polio (IPV), hepatitis A, rabies. Cannot multiply; weaker, so usually needs several doses, sometimes an adjuvant. Vaccination day
Subunit vaccine
 Pieces of the germ or its toxin, e.g. hepatitis B, HPV, tetanus. Trains against those pieces only; usually needs an adjuvant and boosters. Vaccination day
mRNA vaccine
 An antigen recipe in a fat droplet, e.g. Comirnaty (COVID-19). Nearby cells make the antigen; then the mRNA breaks down, never reaching DNA (the cell's records). Vaccination day
Train: what you may notice
Side effects
 Sore arm, tiredness, headache, fever, aches, swollen glands. Mostly the immune response at work, not the disease; none can still mean protection. Within a day, for a few days
Remember: first time slow, next time fast
Memory cells
 Long-lived B and T cells, plus plasma cells still making antibody. On meeting the germ, respond within days, faster and stronger than the first time. Months to decades
Booster
 A later dose of the same or an updated vaccine. Lifts antibodies and memory again; updates follow changing germs like COVID-19. Months to years later

Questions and rules to remember

How long until I am protected? About two weeks after a dose; some vaccines need several doses.
Can a vaccine give me the disease? Non-live vaccines cannot; a live one sometimes causes a mild form.
Why boosters? Antibody levels and memory fade over time, and some germs change.
The more contagious the germ, the more people must be immune for herd immunity: about 95% for measles.
Severe allergic reactions are very rare, about 1 to 10 in a million doses; some clinics ask for a 15-minute wait.
No vaccine protects everyone fully; vaccinated people who still fall ill are usually less sick.
