The picture
Device · A writing desk in one room of your house, where cards are written and answers land; your phone is a pocket-sized one.
Ethernet cable · A private tube from a desk straight to the porter: fast and steady, but only where one has been laid.
Wi-Fi · Cards sent to the porter over the air: no tube needed, but walls weaken it and all rooms share it.
Home router · The porter at the front door, the house's own first sorting office: every card in or out passes him, and he turns away post nobody asked for.
Packet · A postcard of limited size: a long message goes out as many cards that travel on their own, not always by the same road.
IP address · The recipient's and sender's address on every card: inside, the porter gives each desk a number; on cards going out he writes the house's street address instead.
Encryption (TLS) · A secret code your desk and the library agree for each conversation, after the library shows a stamped ID: sorting offices still see the addresses, not the message.
Access line · The lane from your house to the post company, of old copper, TV cable or fast glass fibre, ending at a small box, often built into the porter.
Mobile network · Away from home, your pocket desk sends its cards over the air to a mobile post company's nearest mast, with no porter or lane; that company passes them on like any other.
Internet service provider · The post company you pay: it collects your house's cards, gives the house its street address and passes cards on to other post companies.
DNS · The directory desk, usually the post company's: before any card goes out, it turns a name like "City Library" into the street address to write, and remembers it for a while.
Routers · Sorting offices along the way: each reads the recipient's address and passes the card to the next office closer to it.
Backbone · The motorways between cities and the cables across the sea floor, carrying the cards of millions of houses at once.
Server · The library at the far end: open day and night, it waits for cards and sends the answers back.
On the card: two ways of delivering
TCP · Registered post, arranged between your desk and the library alone: each card is numbered, the library confirms what arrived, missing cards are sent again and all are put back in order.
UDP · Plain post: no numbers, no receipts, no resending, so it is quicker; programs choose it for calls and games, where a late card is useless anyway.
