In the scene on page one
TLS (HTTPS)
Transport Layer Security, formerly SSL
 Encrypts the connection between your browser or app and a server, after checking the server's certificate (4). What you send, from being read or changed on the way. https:// in the address bar (often with a lock), e-mail, apps. Anyone on the same Wi-Fi or network path can read and alter it.
SSH
Secure Shell
 Encrypted remote login to another computer's command line, usually with a key pair; also copies files (scp, sftp). Admin work on servers: commands, passwords and files. Running servers and routers; Git hosting (git@github.com). Telnet, the older tool it replaced, sent passwords as plain text.
VPN
Virtual Private Network, e.g. WireGuard
 An encrypted tunnel that carries all your device's traffic into another network, which treats the device as its own. Traffic on untrusted Wi-Fi; reaching internal systems. Working from home; consumer apps that also mask your location. Internal systems are out of reach, or must face the open internet.
Certificate
X.509, from a certificate authority (CA)
 A digitally signed ID that ties a domain name to the server's public key, issued by a CA your browser trusts. You from a fake server posing as the address you typed. Lock or site icon in the address bar; "certificate expired" warnings. The line is still encrypted, but maybe to the wrong party.
MFA (two-factor)
Multi-factor authentication
 A login with two kinds of proof: something you know (password) plus something you have (phone, key) or are (fingerprint). Your account when the password leaks or is guessed. Codes from an authenticator app (TOTP) or SMS; "Is this you?" prompts. A leaked password is all an attacker needs.
Passkey
FIDO2 / WebAuthn credential
 A key pair per website: you keep the private key, unlocked by fingerprint, face or PIN; the site keeps only the public key. Against phishing: it works only at the real site's address. "Sign in with a passkey" on phone, laptop or security key. You rely on passwords, which can be guessed, reused or phished.
Zero Trust
Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA)
 A security model (NIST SP 800-207), not a protocol: being inside the network earns no trust; every access is checked. Against an intruder who got in once moving on to everything else. Access gateways (ZTNA) that check you and your device every time. "Castle and moat": once past the wall, everything inside is open.
Inside: a pass for a job, or a card that says who you are
OAuth 2.0
Open Authorization
 Lets you grant an app limited access to your data at another service via an access token, without giving it your password. Your password; access is limited (scopes) and can be revoked. "App X wants to access your Google Calendar. Allow?" You'd give the app your password, and with it full access.
OpenID Connect (OIDC)
Built on OAuth 2.0
 The sign-in service also gives the app a signed ID token (a JWT) that states who you are. You from yet another password; the app gets a verified identity. "Sign in with Google / Apple"; work sign-on (often SAML, an older cousin). Each app needs its own account and password.

Rules to remember

  • A lock or https:// means a protected line to that address, not an honest site: check the address too.
  • OAuth hands out permission; OpenID Connect adds who you are. Remove app access you no longer use.
  • Prefer passkeys, which usually count as two factors; else turn on MFA, ideally not by SMS.
  • Private keys (SSH, passkeys) stay on your own devices; only the public half is handed out.
  • A VPN protects the line, not the device: being inside a network earns no automatic trust (Zero Trust).
