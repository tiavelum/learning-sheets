The hillside scene of page one, all zones
No-go zone Within about 45° of the wind on either side. No boat sails straight into the wind. They cannot be set to drive the boat: they flap (luff). The boat slows, stops and drifts back: it is in irons. To get out, back the genoa: sheet it to the side you want the wind on.
Close-hauled About 45° off the wind, as close to it as the boat sails well. Both pulled in tight: the boom near the centreline, the genoa close to the side of the boat. The boat heels most and the wind feels strongest.
Tacking The bow turns through the wind, from close-hauled on one side to close-hauled on the other: about 90°. As the bow crosses the wind, let go the old genoa sheet and pull in the new one. The main swings across by itself. Calls: “Ready about?”, “Ready!”, “Lee-oh!” The boom comes across: keep your head clear.
Close reach About 60° off the wind: between close-hauled and straight from the side. Eased a little from close-hauled. Fast, and still heeling.
Beam reach 90°: straight from the side (on the beam). Eased about halfway; the boom is roughly 45° out. Usually among the fastest and most comfortable courses.
Broad reach About 135° off the wind, from behind the side (over the quarter). Eased well out. The genoa starts to sit in the main’s wind shadow. Fast, with less heel; the wind feels weaker than it is.
Running About 180°: straight from behind. Main out to one side, its boom held forward by a preventer line. The genoa collapses behind the main: furl it, or pole it out on the other side (goose-winged). Slower than a reach, and the boat rolls. An unplanned gybe swings the boom across: keep heads clear.
Gybing The stern turns through the wind, from a broad reach or run on one side to the same on the other. Pull the main in to the centre before the stern crosses the wind, then let it out quickly on the new side. Swap the genoa sheets. Calls: “Ready to gybe?”, “Ready!”, “Gybe-oh!” The boom still crosses with force; heads down.

Angles are typical for a cruising yacht and are measured between the boat’s heading and the direction the wind comes from. Boats differ. In every boat icon the wind comes from the top, the thick line is the mainsail’s boom and the thin line the genoa.

Remember

Where is the wind? Ask it first, every time: flags and ripples show it; the masthead indicator shows the wind you feel, further ahead. Courses are named by the angle to the wind.
The sails sit on the side away from the wind (except a goose-winged genoa on a run). When the wind changes sides in a tack or a gybe, the sails change sides too.
Head up, pull in. Bear away, ease out. Turning toward the wind, pull the sails in; turning away from it, let them out.
Trim by the front edge. Ease a sail until its front edge starts to flap, then pull it in just until the flapping stops.
Tack: bow through the wind. Gybe: stern through the wind. A tack is gentler; in a gybe the boom crosses with force, so sheet in, gybe only on the call and keep heads low.
Stuck in the no-go zone? Back the genoa (sheet it to the side you want the wind on) until the bow falls away.
