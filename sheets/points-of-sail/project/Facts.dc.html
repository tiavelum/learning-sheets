On the hill: where the boat points
No-go zone Any course closer to the wind than about 45° on either side (30–50°, depending on the boat). Cannot fill: they flap (luff) along the middle of the boat. The boat slows, stops and may drift backwards: it is ‘in irons’. To get out, hold the jib out on the windward side, so the wind pushes the bow away from the wind.
Close-hauled About 45° to the wind, just outside the no-go zone. Mainsail and jib sheeted in hard; boom close to the centreline. Most heel and spray, bumpy into the waves. Slower than reaching, but it gains the most ground towards the wind.
Close reach About 60° to the wind, between close-hauled and a beam reach. Eased a little from close-hauled. Still heeled, but faster and more comfortable than close-hauled.
Beam reach About 90°: the wind blows onto the side of the boat (the beam). Eased about halfway out. Fast and steady; often the quickest and most comfortable point of sail.
Broad reach About 135°: the wind comes over the rear corner (the quarter). Eased well out. Fast, with little heel; the boat may roll in following waves.
Running About 180°: the wind comes from straight behind. Main eased right out, nearly at right angles to the boat, sometimes held by a preventer (a rope that stops the boom swinging across); jib often on the other side (goose-winged). Feels calm because the wind seems light, but the boat rolls and is slower than reaching. If the wind comes round to the same side as the boom, it can throw the boom across (an unplanned gybe).
Turning round: moving the wind to the other side
Tacking Turning the bow through the wind, usually from one close-hauled course to the other. Flap as the bow crosses the wind; the old jib sheet is let go, the new one pulled in; the main usually crosses by itself. Calls such as ‘Ready about?’, ‘Ready!’, ‘Lee-ho!’ (or ‘Helm’s a-lee’). Turn smoothly: too fast or too slow loses speed, and the boat can stop in irons.
Gybing Turning the stern through the wind, from a downwind course on one side to one on the other. Mainsheet pulled in to the centre first, then eased out on the new side; the jib is switched over. Calls such as ‘Ready to gybe?’, ‘Ready!’, ‘Gybe-ho!’. The boom still swings across, so keep heads low and out of its path; an unplanned gybe swings it far harder.
Questions and rules to remember

Why not straight into the wind? A sail drives the boat mainly when wind flows across it, like air over a wing (on a run it simply pushes from behind); from straight ahead the sail cannot do this, so it flaps.

Where is the wind? A flag ashore shows the true wind, which every name here counts from. Under way, the wind indicator at the top of the mast shows the apparent wind, which comes from further ahead; trim the sails by that.

Heading up, bearing away. Turning towards the wind (heading up): pull the sheets in. Turning away from it (bearing away): ease them out.

Trim test. Ease a sail until its front edge starts to flutter, then pull it in until the flutter just stops. On a run, ease the main right out instead.

Port and starboard tack. Facing the bow, the wind comes over the left (port) or the right (starboard) side. Tacking and gybing move it to the other side.

Sheets and calls. Sheets are the ropes that trim the sails. Calls differ between skippers: learn your skipper’s before you sail.
